AERIS-1

A compact three-rotor robotics platform with custom embedded control.

TRICOPTER ROBOTICS SHOWCASE

AERIS-1 is a very small Y-tricopter built to show the whole chain of a robot: sense, decide, act.

Two front motors and one rear motor provide lift. The rear motor sits on an SG90 servo that tilts it, which steers the yaw. An ESP32 runs flight code written from scratch. It reads an MPU6500 for motion and a BMP280 for altitude, and drives the motors through two TB6612FNG drivers from a single 1S LiPo battery.

Commands travel from a laptop through an Arduino Uno and an nRF24L01+ radio link. The plan is manual flight first, then a climb to a laptop-set height, a hold, and a controlled descent, tested step by step from 2 m up to 20–30 m. The sensors are verified; flight control and radio control are still being built.

The engineering question

How can a very small aircraft turn sensor data into controlled motion?

  • Sense: measure attitude and pressure continuously.
  • Decide: custom control logic on the ESP32.
  • Act: change motor thrust and rear-rotor angle.

Plate II-B — Control architecture

The electronics are a feedback system.

  • ESP32: reads sensors, receives commands, runs custom flight code.
  • MPU6500 + BMP280: motion and pressure sensing.
  • TB6612FNG × 0: drive the coreless motors.

Why yaw with a servo?

A quadcopter yaws with torque imbalance. This tricopter tilts the rear rotor instead: fewer lifting motors, more mechanical complexity at the tail.

Frequently asked questions

Why a tricopter and not a quadcopter?

A tricopter needs one fewer lifting motor. The trade-off is that the rear rotor has to tilt on a servo to steer yaw, which adds mechanical and control complexity at the tail.

How does it turn?

The rear motor is mounted on an SG90 servo horn. Tilting it angles the thrust sideways, which creates the yawing force. A quadcopter does this with torque differences between its spinning rotors instead.

Is the flight code custom?

Yes. The flight software on the ESP32 is written for this project instead of using a stock flight-controller stack.

How is it controlled?

Commands typed on a laptop go to an Arduino Uno ground station, then over an nRF24L01+ radio link to the ESP32 on the aircraft.

How will it hold altitude?

The BMP280 measures air pressure, and changes in pressure give a relative altitude estimate. Autonomous climb and hold are planned, and not yet working.

How much does it weigh?

The parts weigh 95 g, and the 3D-printed frame is limited to 15 g, so the target is about 110 g.

What works today?

The MPU6500 motion sensor and the BMP280 pressure sensor are verified. Flight control tuning and radio control are in build.

Why start testing at 2 m?

Each height is only attempted after the previous one passes, so any problem shows up where a crash does the least damage.
